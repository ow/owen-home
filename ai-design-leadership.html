AI leadership isn't a tool rollout. It's the work of creating conditions where people can move faster, explore more of the problem, and still meet the quality bar that makes a product trustworthy.

Design teams already know how to experiment. The harder leadership problem is turning scattered experiments into durable organizational leverage. That means deciding where AI belongs in the process, connecting it to the real systems a team uses, and being explicit about the judgment that still belongs to people.

Start with the work, not the technology

I pay attention to the moments where a team's intent gets lost in translation: an idea that takes too long to make tangible, a static mockup that cannot express a complex flow, or a product manager who has to wait for help before testing a small hypothesis. Those are better starting points than a broad mandate to “use AI.”

When I began building Protodash at Stripe, designers were already trying off-the-shelf AI prototyping products. They made it easy to generate something quickly, but the output often looked like a vague imitation of our product. The problem was not a lack of enthusiasm. The tools did not understand the design system, navigation, or product conventions that made a prototype useful in a real review.

The opportunity was specific: help people turn an idea into a realistic Stripe experience without asking them to become front-end engineers first.

Protect the quality bar with real systems

Speed only creates leverage when the result is credible enough to support a decision. Generic components and invented patterns may be fine for a first sketch, but they become a tax when a team has to mentally translate every screen back into the real product.

Protodash connected AI to Stripe's actual design system and wrapped it in opinionated rules. The rules taught the model how the project worked and, just as importantly, what to do when it did not know. A React shell, real components, and predictable infrastructure meant that an AI-generated prototype began much closer to the product's existing quality bar.

This principle applies beyond prototyping. Good AI systems need context, constraints, and a clear definition of quality. Leadership is partly the work of encoding those things so every person does not have to rediscover them in every prompt.

Shorten the distance between an idea and evidence

The most valuable change is not simply producing the same artifact faster. It is changing how often a team can learn. A realistic prototype lets people explore complete flows, empty states, different data, internationalization, and alternate business models before implementation. It makes the work specific enough to test and critique.

That tighter loop changes meetings, too. Feedback can happen inside the experience instead of around a collection of static frames. A team can summarize what it learned, send that context back into the next iteration, and return with something concrete. On one Radar project, a high-fidelity Protodash prototype became the source of truth for engineering. The prototype was no longer a disposable presentation layer; it carried the product intent all the way into implementation.

Prefer demos to memos

Written strategy is useful, but some questions can only be answered by feeling how a product behaves. AI makes it much cheaper to move a discussion from abstract preferences to a working example.

I think of this as a shift toward demos, not memos. A demo reveals missing states, awkward transitions, and disagreements that polished prose can hide. It gives cross-functional teams a shared object to react to. For leaders, building or enabling the demo is often the fastest way to clarify the strategy.

This is also why technical fluency matters in design leadership. I do not need to be the strongest engineer in the room, but understanding systems well enough to shape a tool gives me another way to remove friction for the team. AI lowers the cost of turning that understanding into useful internal products.

Broaden participation without blurring accountability

One of the most encouraging outcomes from Protodash was how quickly product managers adopted it. They could explore ideas, communicate intent, and test earlier without waiting for a designer to translate every thought into a mockup. Designers could spend more time on the taste, structure, and craft that elevated the result.

That does not make every contributor the designer of record. It gives more people a way to participate in the material of the product. Clear ownership and critique still matter. The difference is that the conversation begins with something richer than a paragraph in a document.

The system itself should be participatory, too. Designers can contribute improvements back to Protodash and evolve it around the way they want to work. Adoption is stronger when a tool feels like shared infrastructure rather than a process imposed from above.

Keep judgment human

AI is very good at producing options and maintaining momentum. It is not a substitute for taste, responsibility, or a deep understanding of the customer. Teams still need people to decide which problem matters, notice when an interaction feels wrong, weigh tradeoffs, and hold the work to a coherent standard.

That is the leadership goal I care about: use AI to reduce the mechanical distance between thinking and making, then reinvest the time in better questions, stronger critique, and more considered products. The quality bar should rise because the team can explore more—not fall because generating something became easy.
